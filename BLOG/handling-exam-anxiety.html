Handling Exam Anxiety: A Student's Guide

Not a lecture about "staying positive." Practical, specific things that actually help during the hardest weeks.

13 min read Peer Perspective

Nobody preparing for NEET or JEE needs to be told that these exams are stressful — you're living it. What's usually missing isn't awareness that anxiety exists, it's specific, practical things to do when it shows up in the middle of a mock test, or at 11pm after your parents ask "how's studying going" for the fifth time that week. This guide skips the generic "just relax" advice and gets into what actually helps.

When Panic Hits Mid-Mock Test

You're 45 minutes into a mock, a question you should know suddenly looks unfamiliar, and your chest tightens. Your mind starts racing to every other thing you might not know. This is one of the most common experiences aspirants describe, and there's a reason it happens mid-test specifically: the stakes feel immediate and the clock is visibly running out.

In the moment: Put your pen down. Take four slow breaths, in through your nose for a count of four, out for a count of six. Then physically move to the next question rather than staying stuck. Skipping a stuck question isn't giving up — it's protecting the next 20 questions from being wasted on one that's already draining you.

Afterward, don't just move on from the moment either. When you review that mock later, look specifically at what preceded the panic — was it a genuinely hard question, or a topic you already suspected was weak? That tells you whether the fix is more practice on that topic, or building a habit of skipping and returning rather than freezing.

The Comparison Trap

Someone in your group always seems to be further ahead — more chapters done, higher mock scores, a coaching test rank you don't have. Comparison during exam prep is almost impossible to fully avoid, especially when everyone around you is chasing the same limited seats. But it's worth naming clearly: comparing your day 40 to someone else's day 40 tells you nothing useful, because you don't know their starting point, their resources, or what they're not telling you about their own struggles.

  • Compare your own mock scores over time instead of against a classmate's single result.
  • If a group chat consistently leaves you anxious rather than motivated, it's fine to mute it during your study hours — that's a boundary, not an insult to anyone.
  • Remember that a rank someone shares publicly is one data point in a two-year process, not the whole story.

Managing Parental Pressure Without Blowing Up

Parents asking about your progress usually comes from worry, not malice — but that doesn't make it less exhausting to hear "so how's the syllabus going" every evening. A few approaches that tend to work better than either silent frustration or a full argument:

  1. Give them a scheduled update instead of an on-demand one. A short weekly summary — "this week I covered X, my mock score was Y" — often satisfies the underlying worry and reduces daily check-ins.
  2. Separate their anxiety from your reality. Their questions often reflect their own stress about your future, not an accurate read on how you're actually doing. You're allowed to reassure them without internalizing every worried comment as true.
  3. Ask directly for the kind of support that helps. Something as simple as "I do better when you ask about my week on Sundays rather than every day" is a reasonable thing to say to a parent.

Sleep and Food During the Final Crunch Weeks

In the last stretch before the exam, sleep and meals are usually the first things sacrificed — and it's almost always a bad trade. Sleep deprivation directly hurts memory consolidation, meaning the extra hour you stayed up "revising" often costs you more than it gives back the next day.

Sleep

  • Aim for a consistent sleep and wake time, even on lighter days.
  • Avoid screens for 20-30 minutes before bed if a racing mind is a problem.
  • If you can't fall asleep after 20-30 minutes, get up briefly rather than lying there anxious about not sleeping.

Food

  • Regular mealtimes help regulate energy through long study days better than skipped meals plus snacking.
  • Heavy, greasy meals right before a study block often lead to sluggishness — lighter meals sit better before focused work.
  • Stay hydrated; mild dehydration alone can feel a lot like brain fog and fatigue.
If you notice ongoing sleep problems, loss of appetite, or persistent low mood that isn't easing up, that's worth talking to a parent, school counselor, or doctor about directly — this guide covers everyday exam stress, not a substitute for professional support when things feel heavier than that.

A Few Grounding Reminders

  • One bad mock does not predict your actual exam result — mocks are practice data, not a verdict.
  • Almost everyone around you is anxious too, even the ones who look calm — anxiety just isn't always visible from outside.
  • Taking a real break isn't falling behind — burnout costs far more study time than a planned rest day ever will.

Quick Checklist

  • Do I have a specific plan for mid-mock panic, rather than just hoping it won't happen?
  • Have I set any boundaries around comparison-heavy group chats?
  • Have I talked to my parents about the kind of check-ins that actually help me?
  • Am I protecting sleep and regular meals, especially in the final weeks?

Exam stress is a genuinely sensitive topic, and everyone experiences it differently. If anything you're feeling goes beyond everyday exam nerves, please talk to a trusted adult, school counselor, or doctor — we're happy to help point you toward the right support if you ever need it.
